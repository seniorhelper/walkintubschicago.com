What winter really changes

Anyone who has lived through a Chicago January knows that cold finds its way indoors. For bathing, that shows up in four ways: incoming water is colder, so the water heater has to work harder; bathrooms on outside walls feel chilly; plumbing in exterior walls and unheated spaces can freeze; and the minutes spent seated while a tub fills and drains feel longer.

None of these is a reason to avoid a walk-in tub. Each has a practical answer.

Hot water: the winter bottleneck

A water heater's job is to raise incoming water to a set temperature. In winter, the city water entering your home is much colder than in August, so each gallon takes more heating. A tank that comfortably fills a walk-in tub in summer may run lukewarm by the end of the fill in February.

The U.S. Department of Energy recommends sizing tank water heaters by their first-hour rating, which reflects how much hot water they can deliver in an hour of use. Manufacturers often list a suggested heater size for each walk-in tub model. We compare the two during your quote and, if needed, discuss options like a larger tank or an in-line heater on the tub to hold temperature during a soak.

Simple check: photograph the label on your water heater. Tank size, fuel and age tell us most of what we need to know.

Staying warm in the tub

Warm the room first

Turn the heat up in the bathroom before bathing and close the door. Never use a portable space heater near a tub.

Shorten the waits

Fast-fill faucets and fast drains reduce the time spent seated in a partly full tub. Ask about drain time on any model.

Use the handheld shower

Rinse with warm water while the tub drains. It is the simplest warmth feature there is.

Heated surfaces

Some models offer heated backrests or seats, and in-line heaters keep bath water warm during longer soaks.

Robe within reach

Hang a robe and towel where you can reach them from the seat, before you start.

Mind the radiator

A radiator beside the tub helps warm the room, but can burn. A cover makes it safer.

Preventing frozen pipes

The City of Chicago Department of Water Management publishes freeze-prevention tips every winter. Its guidance includes:

  • Keep proper heat in the home, especially in basements, crawl spaces and closets where water service pipes run.
  • Let warm air circulate wherever pipes run, including under sinks.
  • Insulate pipes, particularly those on outside walls or the perimeter of the home.
  • In extreme cold, run a trickle of cold water at faucets farthest from where water enters the home.
  • Remove garden hoses and protect outdoor faucets.
  • If a pipe freezes, never use an open flame. A hair dryer or heating pad is safer.

Bathrooms on outside walls

Many bungalows and two-flats have the tub on an exterior wall under a window. When the old tub comes out, it is a good moment to check insulation behind the supply lines before the new tub goes in.

Winter moisture and ventilation

In a Chicago winter, a long, hot soak in a closed bathroom fills the air with moisture that condenses on cold windows and exterior walls. Over time, that can lead to peeling paint and mildew. The EPA recommends keeping indoor humidity below 60 percent, ideally between 30 and 50 percent, and using a bathroom exhaust fan that vents to the outdoors during and after bathing.

Many older Chicago bathrooms have no fan at all, only a window that nobody wants to open in January. If yours is one of them, a walk-in tub project is a good time to ask about adding one.

Winter moisture habits

  • Run the fan during the bath and for a while after
  • Wipe condensation off cold windows
  • Leave the tub door open after it drains so it dries

Scheduling a winter installation

Indoor bathroom work can be done year-round. Winter does bring a few practical considerations: icy front steps and porches during delivery, salt and slush tracked in, and the possibility of storms shifting a delivery date. Crews protect floors and the path into the home, and we keep you updated if weather changes the plan.

If the home has only one bathroom, think about timing around holidays and visitors. And if an older adult is recovering from surgery or a fall, coordinate with their discharge plan so the safer bathroom is ready when they need it.

Clear the path: shovel and salt the route from the street to the door, including back porch stairs, before delivery day.

Winter safety for older adults at home

Winter adds fall risks outside the bathroom too: icy steps, dark early evenings and boots that track water onto tile floors. Put a rubber-backed mat at each entry, keep night lights on the route to the bathroom, and dry floors promptly. During severe cold, check on older neighbors and relatives. The City of Chicago opens warming centers during extreme cold, and 311 can help connect residents to services.

Winter questions

Will my water heater fill a walk-in tub in the winter?

It depends on its size, age and recovery rate, and on the tub model. Winter inlet water is colder, so the same tank delivers less comfortable bath water. We compare your heater's label with the tub's recommended heater size.

Can walk-in tubs be installed in winter?

Yes. The work is indoors. Weather can affect delivery days, so clear the route to the door and expect some flexibility on dates during storms.

How do I keep from getting cold while the tub drains?

Warm the room first, rinse with the handheld shower while the water drains, and keep a robe within reach. Fast-drain systems and heated backrests help too.
