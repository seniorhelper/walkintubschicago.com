The short version

Most installs follow the same arc: a conversation, a measure, an order, an install day and a walkthrough. Our worry-free installation service is available nationwide, and for a straightforward alcove swap, one-day installation is often possible. Older plumbing, electrical upgrades, permits, building rules or tricky access can stretch the timeline, and we will tell you up front if we expect that.

If you would rather use your own licensed contractor, we can drop-ship the tub to your home instead. The steps below still apply. Your contractor simply takes on the install-day work.

Step 1: a real conversation

The first call is about the person, not the product. Who will use the tub? How do they get around today, and what do you expect in a few years? Do they want to soak or mostly shower? Is a spouse or caregiver involved? Then we talk about the house: what kind of building, which floor, the size of the current tub and where the water heater lives.

Photos help a lot. A picture of the tub from the doorway, one of the bathroom door, the water heater label and the electrical panel answer most of the early questions.

  • Bather's height, mobility and any equipment they use
  • Building type and floor
  • Current tub size and drain side
  • Water heater size and age
  • Must-have features and budget range

Step 2: measuring and confirming the fit

Chicago bathrooms can be surprising. Walls in a 1920s bungalow may not be square, a greystone bathroom may have a radiator right where a tub door wants to swing, and a high-rise unit may have a service elevator smaller than the box. Before anything is ordered we confirm:

The alcove

Length, depth and height at more than one point, plus which side the drain is on. Many walk-in tubs are built for a standard 60-inch alcove.

The path

Every door, hallway, stair and landing the boxed tub must pass, including back porches and elevators.

Door swing

Whether an outward door has room to open, or whether an inward door makes more sense.

Utilities

Water heater capacity, supply pipe condition and whether a dedicated circuit is needed for jets or heat.

Our measuring guide for Chicago bathrooms explains each measurement.

Step 3: permits and building approval

Inside the City of Chicago, plumbing and electrical work is regulated by the Department of Buildings. The department's Express Permit Program covers many smaller jobs, including repairing or replacing an existing plumbing fixture and certain nonstructural interior alterations in a residential unit. The city requires permitted electrical work to be done by a Chicago-licensed electrical contractor named on the permit.

Whether your project needs a permit, and which type, depends on the scope. Suburbs have their own building departments and rules. Ask your installer who determines this and who pulls the permit.

If you live in a condo, co-op or managed building, expect the association or management to want details first: a scope of work, the contractor's license and insurance certificate, and elevator and work-hour arrangements.

Tip for condo owners: ask management for their contractor requirements as soon as you start shopping. Some boards only meet monthly.

Step 4: install day

  1. Protect and remove

    Floors and the path are covered. The old tub comes out. Cast iron tubs, common in older Chicago homes, are heavy and are often broken up for removal.

  2. Set, level and connect

    The walk-in tub is set level so the door seals and the drain works, then connected to supply and drain lines. Jetted or heated models are wired to their circuit.

  3. Finish and test

    The surround or wall panels are finished, the tub is filled and drained, and the door seal, faucet and any jets are tested.

If the home has only one bathroom, plan for it to be out of service while work is underway. We will help you plan around that.

Step 5: the walkthrough

Before the crew leaves, someone should sit in the tub, dry, and practice: how to step in using the grab bar, how to latch the door, where the controls are, how to start the fill and the drain, and why you stay seated until the water is gone. If an adult child or caregiver helps with bathing, it is worth having them there for this part.

Afterward, keep the model number, drain side and warranty papers in one place, and register the tub with the manufacturer if the warranty requires it.

Call if you notice

  • Water near the door after a bath
  • A latch that gets stiff
  • A drain that slows down
  • A GFCI outlet that keeps tripping

Hiring checklist for any installer

Licensing

Ask who will do the plumbing and the electrical work and for their license numbers. In Chicago, look up licensed contractors through the city; elsewhere in Illinois, ask your village about local requirements.

Scope in writing

Removal, haul-away, plumbing, electrical, wall finish, permits and cleanup should each be listed.

Warranty in writing

Know what covers the tub, the door seal, motors and labor, and for how long.

No pressure

A fair company gives you time to decide. Under the FTC Cooling-Off Rule, many sales made in your home can be canceled within three business days.

Installation questions

Can a walk-in tub really be installed in one day in Chicago?

For a straightforward swap into an existing alcove with workable plumbing, one-day installation is often possible. Permits, building approvals, electrical upgrades, old pipes or difficult access can add time, and we discuss that before scheduling.

Do I need a permit to install a walk-in tub in Chicago?

It depends on the scope of work. The Department of Buildings' Express Permit Program covers many fixture replacements and interior alterations. Electrical work for jets or heaters must be done by a Chicago-licensed electrical contractor. Your installer should confirm what your job needs.

Can you get a walk-in tub up the stairs of a two-flat?

Usually, with planning. We measure the stairway, landings and doors, including the back porch route if that is easier. Some compact models are chosen specifically because they travel well through tight spaces.
