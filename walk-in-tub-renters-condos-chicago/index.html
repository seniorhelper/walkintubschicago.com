Short answer: Usually, yes. The Fair Housing Act and the Illinois Human Rights Act let a tenant with a disability make reasonable changes to an apartment at the tenant's own cost, and Illinois condo law tells boards to reasonably accommodate owners with disabilities. A landlord can ask for a description, permits and good workmanship, and may ask you to restore the interior later.

Can a Chicago landlord say no to a walk-in tub or roll-in shower?

Not just because they would rather you didn't. Under the federal Fair Housing Act, it is unlawful for a housing provider to refuse a reasonable modification that a person with a disability needs to fully use their home. Illinois says the same thing in its own words: Section 3-102.1 of the Illinois Human Rights Act makes it a civil rights violation to refuse "reasonable modifications of existing premises" paid for by the person with a disability when they are needed for full enjoyment of the home.

Two conditions sit inside that rule. The change has to connect to a disability, so a tenant who simply wants a nicer bathroom is not covered. And it has to be reasonable. A walk-in tub that fits the existing 60 inch alcove, or a roll-in shower built where the tub was, is the kind of interior change the federal guidance itself uses as an example: the 2008 joint statement from HUD and the Department of Justice walks through a tenant installing a roll-in shower.

What the law gives a landlord is the right to set sensible conditions, not a veto. Here is where the line falls, according to the joint statement and the Illinois statute.

Who pays for the change?

It depends on who owns the building and whether it gets federal money. In ordinary private housing, the tenant pays. In public housing and other federally assisted buildings, Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act generally puts the cost on the housing provider unless it would be an undue burden, or the tenant can move to a unit that already works. The Illinois Department of Human Rights adds a detail that trips up many families: a tenant who rents a private apartment with a Housing Choice Voucher is still in the "tenant pays" group, because the voucher does not make the building federally assisted for this purpose.

"Tenant pays" does not always mean paying alone. Chicago's HomeMod program, run by the Mayor's Office for People with Disabilities, accepts renters who have the landlord's prior approval and lists roll-in showers among its typical work. Will you have to put the old tub back when you move out?

Maybe. In a rental, Illinois lets a landlord make restoration a condition of permission "where it is reasonable to do so," with reasonable wear and tear excepted. The federal guidance uses grab bars to show what reasonable looks like: asking a tenant to take the bars down at move-out is fine, but asking them to rip out the hidden blocking inside the wall is not, because the blocking does not affect the next tenant.

Escrow is the other piece. A landlord cannot raise the security deposit, but may negotiate an interest-bearing escrow paid in over a reasonable period, only when it is needed to be reasonably sure the restoration money will be there. The amount cannot exceed the cost of restoring, and the interest belongs to the tenant. The joint statement says landlords may not require escrow as a routine matter; it looks at things like how big the change is, how long the lease runs and the tenant's history.

Practical points our installers see play out in Chicago two-flats and courtyard buildings:

  • A walk-in tub in the old tub's spot is the easiest to undo. It uses the same alcove and drain area, so putting a standard tub back is a swap, not a rebuild.
  • A roll-in shower changes more. Lowering the entry and setting a new base is a bigger restoration job, so settle the restoration question in writing before work starts.
  • Some owners decide to keep it. An accessible bathroom can be a selling point for the next tenant. If the landlord agrees to waive restoration, get that in the same letter as the approval.

What can a condo board require in Chicago?

If you own the unit, the question shifts from permission to approval. Under the Illinois Condominium Property Act, Section 18.4(q), a board must reasonably accommodate the needs of a unit owner with a disability, as the federal Civil Rights Act of 1968, the Illinois Human Rights Act and local ordinances require, when it approves modifications in a unit or decides how common elements are used. The HUD and DOJ statement lists condominium associations among the housing providers the reasonable modification rule covers.

Reasonable accommodation still leaves the board its normal job. Plumbing risers, the concrete slab under a high-rise bathroom and the walls between units are usually common elements, so the board will want to know exactly what will be touched. Expect to provide:

  • The model and its spec sheet, with the footprint, weight when full and drain location.
  • An itemized scope showing whether the slab, risers or shared walls are involved.
  • The contractor's license and insurance documents, and the permit plan.
  • The work date, any water shutoff, and the service elevator reservation if the building has one.

On concrete, recessing a shower into the slab is rarely approved, which is why condo projects usually use a low-profile shower base with a collapsible water dam. Our Chicago walk-in showers guide compares the threshold options, and the measuring guide covers elevator cabs and stair turns.

How do you ask, step by step?

  1. Get the need in writing. A short note from a doctor or occupational therapist that ties the tub or shower to the disability is usually enough. You do not need to hand over your medical records.
  2. Get an itemized quote and spec sheet for the exact model, so the request describes real work, not an idea.
  3. Send a written request to the landlord, property manager or board. Keep a copy and note the date.
  4. Answer reasonable conditions on permits, workmanship and restoration, and put any restoration or escrow agreement in writing.
  5. Schedule only after written approval. If you are also applying to a program such as HomeMod or VA HISA, wait for that approval too.

A request you can adapt

"I live at [address], unit [number]. Because of a disability, I need to replace the bathtub with [a walk-in tub / a roll-in shower] so I can bathe without stepping over the tub wall. A note from my [doctor or therapist] is attached. I will pay for the work. The attached quote and spec sheet describe the model and scope. The installer will obtain any required permits and complete the work in a workmanlike manner. Please let me know in writing whether you approve, and whether you will ask me to restore the original tub when I move out."

Fill in the brackets and keep it factual. This is a template, not legal advice.

If a landlord or board refuses outright, delays without answering, or attaches conditions the law does not allow, you can contact the Illinois Department of Human Rights or file a housing discrimination complaint with HUD. A fair housing organization or legal aid office can tell you which route fits.

Tub or shower, when you do not own the walls?

Choose by the person first, then by how much the building lets you change. A walk-in tub suits someone who can walk in, sit steadily and wants to soak, and in a rental it is the easier change to reverse. A roll-in shower suits someone who uses a shower wheelchair or bathes with a helper, and it tends to need more floor work. Renter and condo questions

Can my landlord refuse to let me install a walk-in tub?

Not if the change is reasonable and needed because of a disability. The Fair Housing Act and the Illinois Human Rights Act require landlords to allow reasonable modifications paid for by the tenant. The landlord may ask for a description of the work, permits and good workmanship.

Who pays for a roll-in shower in a rented apartment?

In private housing, the tenant pays, including tenants who rent with a Housing Choice Voucher. In public housing and other federally assisted buildings, the provider generally pays under Section 504 unless the cost is an undue burden.

Will I have to remove the walk-in tub when I move out?

Your landlord may require you to restore the interior where that is reasonable, minus normal wear and tear. Ask before work starts and get the answer in writing. A walk-in tub set in the original tub space is usually the simplest change to reverse.

Can my landlord raise my security deposit for a bathroom modification?

No. Illinois law says a landlord may not increase the customary security deposit for a person with a disability. A landlord may negotiate an interest-bearing escrow for restoration only when it is needed, and it cannot exceed the restoration cost.

Does my Chicago condo board have to approve a walk-in tub?

Boards still review plumbing work, but Section 18.4(q) of the Illinois Condominium Property Act requires them to reasonably accommodate owners with disabilities when approving changes in a unit. Bring the spec sheet, an itemized scope, contractor documents and the permit plan.
